The premise

AI makes teachers more important, not less.

AI is good at exactly the parts that were never teaching: the second round of comments, the worksheet retyped at three levels, the fortieth report card sentence. Hand those over. What remains is the work only you can do.

Where the week goes

Count the hours. Then take them back.

The cases teachers bring us most, and what happens once the workflow is built. The work doesn't get worse. It gets more personal, because saved time goes into judgment, not typing.

Essay feedback

A weekend of marking → one evening

120 essays, each with specific comments tied to your rubric, in your voice, referencing the student's actual sentences. You review and sharpen every one instead of writing every one.

Differentiation

Three versions of everything → one prompt

The same worksheet at three reading levels, with the same learning goal, used to mean building it three times. Now it means building it once and reviewing the variants.

Report season

Forty report cards → an afternoon

Start from your term's notes and grades for each student. Draft comments that sound like you, are specific to each child, and never repeat the same sentence forty times.

Personalized learning, for real this time

One classroom, thirty students, thirty versions.

You already know which student needs a simpler text, who is bored, and who keeps making the same mistake. What was missing was the time to act on it. This is where AI changes the classroom, not just the paperwork.

Leveled materials from one source

Take tomorrow's reading and produce versions for your strongest readers, your middle band, and your EAL students, all carrying the same content and the same discussion questions. Nobody gets a "different" lesson. Everybody gets a reachable one.

Practice built from each student's mistakes

Paste in what a student actually got wrong this week and generate a practice set aimed at exactly that misconception, at the right difficulty, with worked solutions. Ten students, ten different sets, minutes each.

Feedback that quotes the student back

Comments that point at the student's own second paragraph, not generic praise. Set the rubric and the tone once; every student gets feedback that reads like you sat with their draft, because in the minutes you save, you actually can.

The fast finishers and the strugglers, both served

Extension problems for the student who finished in ten minutes. A scaffolded version for the one who froze. Translations and glossaries for the family that reads in another language. The edges of the class stop being an afterthought.

Every session is hands on keyboards, in a real room, on your real work. This is what ours look like.

From our sessions

Three teachers, three afternoons, three tools.

From the faculty trainings we've run. Different schools, same pattern: bring a real task, leave with it working.

Science

The simulation she couldn't buy

A biology teacher described the osmosis demonstration she wished existed: sliders for concentration, a cell that swells and shrinks, questions that appear as students experiment. Forty minutes later it was running in a browser, built from her plain-English description, no code written by her. Her students used it that week.

English

Feedback for every draft, not every third one

An English teacher with five sections used to rotate which classes got written comments, because there was one of her. In the session she encoded her rubric and her commenting style. Now every draft gets specific, teacher-reviewed feedback, and her evenings hold conferences with the students who need her most.

Homeroom

Parent letters in two languages

A homeroom teacher at an international school drafted her weekly family update, then produced a Chinese version that kept her warmth instead of reading like a manual. Families who used to skim a foreign-language email now reply. Total added time: about four minutes a week.

Fair questions

The doubts we hear, answered straight.

I'm not technical. Will I be left behind in the room?+

The sessions are designed for teachers who have never opened these tools, and the teachers who arrive most skeptical tend to leave with the most. Everything is plain language: if you can explain a task to a student teacher, you can run it here.

Won't AI-written feedback feel generic to my students?+

Generic output is what you get from generic prompting, and that's exactly what we train out. Your rubric, your tone, the student's own sentences quoted back: you set the standard, review every comment, and stay the author. The tool drafts; you judge.

My students already use AI to cheat. Doesn't this make it worse?+

Pretending the tools don't exist is what makes it worse. One of our six core cases is assessment design after AI: tasks that measure thinking when every student has the same tools you do. Teachers who understand the tools set better assignments than teachers who ban them.

Our school restricts which tools we can use.+

Common, and workable. We adapt every session to the tools your school has approved, and the workflows transfer: the skill is describing your task precisely, not any one product. We're happy to talk with your IT lead beforehand.

Will any of this survive contact with next term?+

This is why we insist you bring real tasks. A tool built on your actual marking load gets used next week, because next week the load returns. You keep every prompt and template, and school-wide trainings include follow-up for the teachers who want to go further.

The hours you get back go straight to the part of teaching that matters.
