This summer, ISB hosted the AI Founders Program: five days in August for students in grades 7 to 12, selected by application. No coding experience was required. The promise was simple: learn AI skills, start a company, and build something real, a live product at its own link by the final afternoon. Mentors were in the room every day, and every team demoed what worked.

Students don’t study startups. They run one, in ten stages.

  3. 03Form the teamBuilder, designer, storyteller

  5. 05Design itScreens, type, and color
  6. 06Build itDirect AI tools to build it
  7. 07Ship it liveA public link of its own
  8. 08Test and iterateWatch users, then rebuild
  9. 09Brand itA name, a logo, and a story
  10. 10Pitch and sellSet a price, pitch it live
Students don't study startups; they run one. Every student walks the same ten stages real companies run on, and each stage teaches one move with the AI tools professionals use for it. Find a real problem and sharpen it into one sentence. Validate it by talking to real people before building anything. Form a team with roles. Cut the idea down to the smallest version worth shipping. Design it. (Click.) Then build it by directing AI tools, ship it to a public link, watch real users and rebuild, brand it, and finally set a price and pitch it live.

The research · two 2025 studies

Used as a shortcut, AI does the thinking. Set up as a coach, it helps students learn more.

High school math · nearly 1,000 students
−17%

exam grades after practicing with plain ChatGPT. A tutor version that gave hints, not answers, largely avoided the drop.

Harvard physics · a randomized trial
2×

the learning gains of an active-learning class, in less time, with an AI tutor built on the same methods

Bastani and others, PNAS, June 25, 2025: grades fell 17% once plain ChatGPT was taken away; with safeguards, the harm was “largely mitigated”; Kestin and others, Scientific Reports, June 3, 2025: 194 Harvard physics students, median learning gains more than double

This is the one piece of research we'll show, because it answers the worry every teacher has. In a study of nearly a thousand high school students, those who practiced math with plain ChatGPT did 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. They used it as a crutch. But a second group used a version set up like a tutor, giving hints instead of answers, and the harm largely disappeared. And at Harvard, a randomized trial found students learned about twice as much, in less time, from an AI tutor built on good teaching methods. So the question isn't whether students use AI. It's how their use of it is designed. That's a teaching question, and it's what the week was built around.

Name the scarce skills

Agency chooses, taste judges, and craft finishes.

The three skills
  1. 01AgencyDecide what is worth building before anyone assigns it.
  2. 02TasteJudge whether the output is actually good.
  3. 03CraftFinish it so people can trust it.
We give the scarce part three names. Agency is deciding what is worth building before anyone assigns it. Taste is judging whether the output is actually good. Craft is finishing it so people can trust it. AI can help with every step, but it can't do these three for a student. This is also our answer to the parents' question: these are the skills that outlast the tools.

How the week was designed

Each skill had a moment in the week that students couldn’t skip.

Skill · how the week trains it
  1. 01AgencyStudents pitch their own idea in three minutes, with no slides, and choose the problem on day one.
  2. 02TasteA prototype is online by midweek, so real users and mentors react to it every day.
  3. 03CraftBuilding stops at a fixed hour, and students explain every decision live on demo day.
This is the design. Agency: on day one, students pitch their own idea in three minutes, with no slides, and choose the problem themselves, so nobody can hand them the answer. Taste: a working prototype is online at a public link by midweek, so real users and mentors react to it every day, and students learn which version actually works. Craft: building stops at a fixed hour, and on demo day every student explains each decision live. AI is in every step, but the thinking stays with the student.

Is using AI just cheating?

No. AI tools write the code, and students make every decision.

AI tools and mentors handle
  • Writing the code
  • Putting it online
  • Fixing technical errors
Students decide
  • What to build, and for whom
  • Which version is better, and why
  • When it’s good enough to ship
The question we get most from teachers: isn't this just cheating? No. AI tools and mentors handle the technical complexity: writing the code, putting it online, fixing errors. Students handle the product: what to build and for whom, which version is better and why, and when it's good enough to ship. On demo day they explain every decision live. That judgment is the part that compounds, and it's the part teachers already know how to coach.

What students need from us

Skills grow from practice with structure, so your role becomes the coach.

  1. {{ic:folder-kanban}}A real projectwith something at stake
  2. {{ic:search-check}}A coachwho asks how you checked it
  3. {{ic:messages-square}}Honest feedbackfrom real people
The tools are everywhere. What's scarce is the structure around them: a real project, someone who asks how you checked it, and honest feedback. None of that is technical. It's what good teachers already do, and it's exactly what the mentors did all week. With AI in the room, the teacher moves from the person with the answers to the coach who asks the questions.

Try this on Monday · three coaching questions

Three questions turn any AI task into practice in agency, taste, and craft.

  1. {{ic:compass}}“Why this problem?”Agency: they choose, not the AI
  2. {{ic:search-check}}“How do you know it’s good?”Taste: they judge what it made
  3. {{ic:badge-check}}“What would make someone trust it?”Craft: they check and finish it
Here's something you can use on Monday, in any subject, with any tool your school allows. When a student shows you something made with AI, ask three questions. Why this problem? That's agency. How do you know it's good? That's taste. What would make someone trust it? That's craft. You don't need to know how the tool works to ask any of them.
