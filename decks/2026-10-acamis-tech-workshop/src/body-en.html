The worry · two 2025 studies

The worry is fair: when AI does the thinking, students learn less.

MIT Media Lab · essay writing
83%

of ChatGPT users couldn’t quote a sentence from the essay they had just written, against 11% without it

High school math · a field experiment
−17%

exam grades once unrestricted ChatGPT was taken away, against students who never had it

Kosmyna and others, MIT Media Lab, “Your Brain on ChatGPT”, June 2025: 54 adults writing essays, a preprint not yet peer-reviewed; Bastani and others, PNAS, June 25, 2025: nearly 1,000 high school math students

Let's say the worry out loud. In the MIT study, people who wrote essays with ChatGPT mostly couldn't quote a single sentence of what they had just turned in. It's a small study and not yet peer-reviewed, but it matches what teachers see. The second study is larger: nearly a thousand high school students practiced math with plain ChatGPT. Their practice scores went up, but when it was taken away for the exam, they did 17% worse than students who never had it. They used it as a crutch. So yes, used this way, AI does the thinking for them. But the same study has a second finding.

The difference · how the AI is set up

Design is the difference: AI that coaches instead of answering helps students learn more.

Same math study · an AI tutor
No drop

in exam grades when the AI was set up to give hints, not answers

Harvard physics · a randomized trial
2×

the learning gains of an active-learning class, in less time, with an AI tutor built on the same methods

Bastani and others, PNAS, 2025: with safeguards, the harm was “largely mitigated”; Kestin and others, Scientific Reports, June 3, 2025: 194 Harvard physics students, median learning gains more than double

Same study: a second group used a version of the AI set up like a tutor. It gave hints, not answers. That group's exam grades held up; the harm largely disappeared. And at Harvard, a randomized trial in a physics course found students learned about twice as much, in less time, from an AI tutor built on the same teaching methods as the class. So the question isn't whether students use AI. It's who designs how they use it. That is a teaching question, and it's yours. Here's how we designed one week.

Name the scarce skills

Agency chooses, taste judges, and craft finishes.

The three skills
  1. 01AgencyDecide what is worth building before anyone assigns it.
  2. 02TasteJudge whether the output is actually good.
  3. 03CraftFinish it so people can trust it.
We give the scarce part three names. Agency is deciding what is worth building before anyone assigns it. Taste is judging whether the output is actually good. Craft is finishing it so people can trust it. AI can help with every step, but it can't do these three for a student.

How the week was designed

Each day of the week trains one of the three skills.

Skill · when · how
  1. 01AgencyDay 1. Students choose the problem. Nobody assigns it.
  2. 02TasteDays 2 and 4. Real users and a real price test whether it’s good.
  3. 03CraftDays 3 and 5. A live link and demo day mean finishing it for real.
This is the design. Day one trains agency: students choose the problem, so nobody can hand them the answer. Days two and four train taste: users tell them what isn't working, and setting a price forces them to ask whether it's actually worth anything. Days three and five train craft: a live link and a demo day mean the product has to work for someone else. AI is in every step, but the thinking stays with the student.

What students need from us

Skills grow from practice with structure, so your role becomes the coach.

  1. {{ic:folder-kanban}}A real projectwith something at stake
  2. {{ic:search-check}}A coachwho asks how you checked it
  3. {{ic:messages-square}}Honest feedbackfrom real people
The tools are everywhere. What's scarce is the structure around them: a real project, someone who asks how you checked it, and honest feedback. None of that is technical. It's what good teachers already do. With AI in the room, the teacher moves from the person with the answers to the coach who asks the questions.

Try this on Monday · three coaching questions

Three questions turn any AI task into practice in agency, taste, and craft.

  1. {{ic:compass}}“Why this problem?”Agency: they choose, not the AI
  2. {{ic:search-check}}“How do you know it’s good?”Taste: they judge what it made
  3. {{ic:badge-check}}“What would make someone trust it?”Craft: they check and finish it
Here's something you can use on Monday, in any subject, with any tool your school allows. When a student shows you something made with AI, ask three questions. Why this problem? That's agency. How do you know it's good? That's taste. What would make someone trust it? That's craft. You don't need to know how the tool works to ask any of them.
