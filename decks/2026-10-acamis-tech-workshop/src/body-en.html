Teaching philosophy

Staying competitive with AI takes three human skills.
Without them, AI does the thinking.

01Agency

Choosing what is worth doing, before anyone assigns it.

Without itAI picks the problem, and learners follow.

02Taste

Judging whether the output is actually good.

Without itLearners accept the first answer AI gives.

03Craft

Finishing the work so people can trust it.

Without itThe work stays an unchecked first draft.

This is how we teach every learner, from students to teachers and parents, and the lens for today.

Parents and teachers ask us the same thing: what does it take for children to stay competitive in a world with AI? Three very human skills that AI can't supply, and that students have to practice. Agency: choosing what is worth doing before anyone assigns it. Without it, AI picks the problem and learners just follow. Taste: judging whether the output is actually good. Without it, learners accept the first answer AI gives them. Craft: finishing the work so people can trust it. Without it, the work stays an unchecked first draft. This is our teaching philosophy at Arena School, and it applies to every learner we teach: students, and also teachers and parents. If learners can't do these three things, AI can be a negative force in their learning. Keep these three in mind for everything you see today.

Why the design matters · two 2025 studies

Used as a shortcut, AI does the thinking. Set up as a coach, it helps students learn more.

High school math · nearly 1,000 students
−17%

exam grades after practicing with plain ChatGPT. A tutor version that gave hints, not answers, largely avoided the drop.

Harvard physics · a randomized trial
2×

the learning gains of an active-learning class, in less time, with an AI tutor built on the same methods

Bastani and others, PNAS, June 25, 2025: grades fell 17% once plain ChatGPT was taken away; with safeguards, the harm was “largely mitigated”; Kestin and others, Scientific Reports, June 3, 2025: 194 Harvard physics students, median learning gains more than double

This is the one piece of research we'll show, because it answers the worry every teacher has. In a study of nearly a thousand high school students, those who practiced math with plain ChatGPT did 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. They used it as a crutch. But a second group used a version set up like a tutor, giving hints instead of answers, and the harm largely disappeared. And at Harvard, a randomized trial found students learned about twice as much, in less time, from an AI tutor built on good teaching methods. So the question isn't whether students use AI. It's how their use of it is designed. That's a teaching question, and it's what our learning framework is built to answer.

Looking back, what made the difference on demo day was not the tools. Every team had the same tools. It was three skills, and the week gave each one a moment students could not skip. Agency is deciding what is worth building before anyone assigns it. On day one, each student chooses the problem and pitches it in three minutes, with no slides, so nobody can hand them the answer. Taste is judging whether the output is actually good, and being able to say why. By midweek the prototype is online at a public link, real users try it and get confused, and mentors react to it every day. That's how students learn which version actually works. Craft is finishing it well enough that people can trust it. Building stops at a fixed hour each day, so teams demo only what works, and on demo day every student explains each decision live. AI is in every step, but these three stay with the student. The longer version is on our site, at arenaschool.org/approach.

So far it has been about students. Now it is about you, because none of that works without teachers having time for it. Two numbers. First, a randomized trial in England: teachers who used AI for lesson planning spent 31% less time on it, and blind reviewers rated the plans the same. (Click.) Second, a 2025 Gallup survey of more than two thousand US teachers: those who use AI every week estimate it saves them almost six hours a week. Over a school year, that's about six weeks. (Click.) Here's what that looks like in practice. An English teacher we spoke with built himself a simple grading timer. It doesn't grade anything. He still reads every paper and writes every comment himself. What it does is time each paper, so every student gets an equal share of his attention, nobody rushed at the end of the pile and nobody over-weighted at the start, and his marking stays inside a set time. He's more accountable and more productive. That's the pattern: AI handles the housekeeping, and the teacher keeps the judgment.

The question we get most from teachers: isn't this just cheating? No. Look at who does what. AI tools and mentors handle the technical complexity: writing the code, putting it online, fixing errors. Students make the product decisions: which problem, and for whom; what to cut once real users have tried it; and the name, the price, and the pitch. AI can't make those calls for them, and on demo day they explain every one of them live. That judgment is the part that compounds, and it's the part teachers already know how to coach.
