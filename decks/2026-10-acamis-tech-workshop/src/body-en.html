Using those tools, students don't study startups; they run one. The week walks every student through the same ten stages real companies run on, and the small icons are the tools used at each stage. Day one, they pick a problem: find the idea, validate it by talking to real people, form a team with roles, and cut the idea down to a one-page plan. They leave with that plan and a first prototype. Day two, they go public: design it, build it by directing AI tools, and ship it to a public link. Real people try it, don't understand it, and the team fixes it. Day three is the rebuild: they watch real users and rebuild the weak parts. Most strong projects look nothing like the day-one plan, and that's the point. Day four is polish and price: a name, a logo, a landing page, and a real price. Day five is demo day: two-minute live pitches to a panel of judges and to families.

The research · two 2025 studies

Used as a shortcut, AI does the thinking. Set up as a coach, it helps students learn more.

High school math · nearly 1,000 students
−17%

exam grades after practicing with plain ChatGPT. A tutor version that gave hints, not answers, largely avoided the drop.

Harvard physics · a randomized trial
2×

the learning gains of an active-learning class, in less time, with an AI tutor built on the same methods

Bastani and others, PNAS, June 25, 2025: grades fell 17% once plain ChatGPT was taken away; with safeguards, the harm was “largely mitigated”; Kestin and others, Scientific Reports, June 3, 2025: 194 Harvard physics students, median learning gains more than double

This is the one piece of research we'll show, because it answers the worry every teacher has. In a study of nearly a thousand high school students, those who practiced math with plain ChatGPT did 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away. They used it as a crutch. But a second group used a version set up like a tutor, giving hints instead of answers, and the harm largely disappeared. And at Harvard, a randomized trial found students learned about twice as much, in less time, from an AI tutor built on good teaching methods. So the question isn't whether students use AI. It's how their use of it is designed. That's a teaching question, and it's what the week was built around.

At Arena School we give the scarce part three names, and we build every program around them. Agency is deciding what is worth building before anyone assigns it. On day one, each student chooses the problem and pitches it in three minutes, with no slides, so nobody can hand them the answer. Taste is judging whether the output is actually good, and being able to say why. By midweek the prototype is online at a public link, real users try it and get confused, and mentors react to it every day. That's how students learn which version actually works. Craft is finishing it well enough that people can trust it. Building stops at a fixed hour each day, so teams demo only what works, and on demo day every student explains each decision live. AI is in every step, but these three stay with the student. The longer version is on our site, at arenaschool.org/approach.

Is using AI just cheating?

No. AI tools write the code, and students make every decision.

AI tools and mentors handle
  • Writing the code
  • Putting it online
  • Fixing technical errors
Students decide
  • Which problem, and for whom
  • What to cut after users try it
  • The name, the price, and the pitch
The question we get most from teachers: isn't this just cheating? No. Look at who does what. AI tools and mentors handle the technical complexity: writing the code, putting it online, fixing errors. Students make the product decisions: which problem, and for whom; what to cut once real users have tried it; and the name, the price, and the pitch. AI can't make those calls for them, and on demo day they explain every one of them live. That judgment is the part that compounds, and it's the part teachers already know how to coach.

So what do students need from us? The tools are everywhere. What's scarce is the structure around them: a real project with something at stake, honest feedback from real people, and someone who asks how they checked it. None of that is technical. It's what good teachers already do, and it's exactly what the mentors did all week. With AI in the room, the teacher moves from the person with the answers to the coach who asks the questions. And here's something you can use on Monday, in any subject, with any tool your school allows. When a student shows you something made with AI, ask three questions. Why this problem? That's agency. How do you know it's good? That's taste. What would make someone trust it? That's craft. You don't need to know how the tool works to ask any of them. Here's how it works. Step one: call out something from your school week. A workflow you repeat, like rewriting the same parent email every Friday, or a problem you keep running into, like tracking which students have turned in drafts. We'll take a few ideas and the room picks one. Step two: we build it live with Claude Code, an AI assistant from Anthropic that writes the code for a website or a small web app from plain English. We type what you said, almost word for word, and in a few minutes there's a first version on screen. Step three is the important part: we open it together and judge it. Does it actually solve the problem? Is it clear? Would you use it on Monday? Every fix is one more plain sentence, and we keep going until the room says it's good enough. It's the same loop your students would run: choose, judge, and finish. When we're done, we publish it; the link to what we built is on the session page, under slides and resources. Logistics: Claude Code needs a working internet connection, so confirm the venue network, or bring a phone hotspot, before the session starts. Keep a recorded backup of a full build open in another tab; if the network drops, switch to the recording and keep narrating the same three steps.
