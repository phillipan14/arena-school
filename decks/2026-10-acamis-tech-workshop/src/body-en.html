Three human skills that AI can’t supply. (Click.) Agency: choosing what is worth doing before anyone assigns it. Without it, AI picks the problem and the learner follows. (Click.) Taste: judging whether the output is actually good. Without it, learners take the first answer AI gives. (Click.) Craft: finishing the work so people can trust it. Without it, the work stays an unchecked first draft. (Click.) This is our philosophy for every learner, and the lens for the hour. We teach the tools, but the most important part, and the reason this has to happen in person, is the human part. Agency: are you still thinking for yourself? Taste: can you tell whether what AI made is good or bad? Craft: it isn’t a one-shot prompt; it’s building and iterating, again and again, sometimes a hundred times. We’re not teaching prompt engineering. We’re teaching how to use AI the right way. I’ll name the three skills once, here; from now on you’ll see them in action. And this isn’t only our opinion.

Two studies from 2025. Nearly a thousand high school students practiced math with plain ChatGPT, and scored 17% worse once it was taken away. A tutor version that gave hints instead of answers largely avoided the drop. (Click.) At Harvard, a randomized trial found students learned about twice as much, in less time, from an AI tutor built on good teaching methods. Same technology, opposite results. The difference is the design, and the design is what our framework is for.

This is the Arena School learning framework. It has four strands, it is built on project-based learning, and it runs as a cycle. At the center, the learner does the thinking; around the edge are the four conditions: in-person feedback, AI use disclosed and cited, learner voice and choice, and an authentic audience. Read each card top to bottom: what the learner decides, where AI helps, and what the teacher asks. (Click.) Inquiry and planning: the learner picks the problem and who it’s for; AI can suggest angles; the teacher asks, why this problem? (Click.) Research and evaluation: AI searches and summarizes; the learner decides what to trust; the teacher asks, how did you check it? (Click.) Creating and communicating: AI writes the code, the drafts, and the visuals; the learner decides what to build and what to cut. (Click.) Collaboration and reflection: AI can give a first round of feedback; the learner decides what to change after real critique. Then the next question starts the cycle again. In every card, AI does more of the making, and the decision stays with the learner. We say learner on purpose: the same framework runs our student programs, our teacher training, and our curriculum work.

Here are the learning outcomes, written so a teacher could assess them on any project. Inquiry and planning: frame a researchable question; plan with milestones and adjust. Research and evaluation: find, evaluate, and cite sources, including checking AI output; compare options against criteria; and understand AI itself, how it works, where it fails, and the bias and privacy issues it raises. Creating and communicating: build and test a product, prototype, or solution, deciding which steps to hand to AI and disclosing how they used it; write a clear argument in their own words; present and defend their reasoning to an authentic audience. Collaboration and reflection: work in a team with shared roles; give and use critique; and reflect on their process. (Click.) A note on the word learners: these outcomes apply to every program we run, for students, teachers, school leaders, or parents. The outcomes stay the same; the depth grows, from middle school to high school and on to adults.

Backup · looking back on the week

The ISB week gave each skill
a moment students couldn’t skip.

  1. 01Agency

    Choosing what is worth doing, before anyone assigns it.

    How the week trained it

    On day one, each student chooses the problem and pitches it in three minutes, with no slides.

  2. 02Taste

    Judging whether the output is actually good.

    How the week trained it

    By midweek, a prototype is online at a public link, and real users and mentors react every day.

  3. 03Craft

    Finishing the work so people can trust it.

    How the week trained it

    Building stops at a fixed hour each day, and on demo day students explain every decision live.

Looking back, what made the difference on demo day was not the tools. Every team had the same tools. It was three skills, and the week gave each one a moment students could not skip. Agency is deciding what is worth building before anyone assigns it. On day one, each student chooses the problem and pitches it in three minutes, with no slides, so nobody can hand them the answer. Taste is judging whether the output is actually good, and being able to say why. By midweek the prototype is online at a public link, real users try it and get confused, and mentors react to it every day. That's how students learn which version actually works. Craft is finishing it well enough that people can trust it. Building stops at a fixed hour each day, so teams demo only what works, and on demo day every student explains each decision live. AI is in every step, but these three stay with the student. The longer version is on our site, at arenaschool.org/approach.

Backup · is using AI just cheating?

No. AI tools write the code,
and students make every decision.

AI tools and mentors handle
  • Writing the code
  • Putting it online
  • Fixing technical errors
Students decide
  • Which problem, and for whom
  • What to cut after users try it
  • The name, the price, and the pitch
The question we get most from teachers: isn't this just cheating? No. Look at who does what. AI tools and mentors handle the technical complexity: writing the code, putting it online, fixing errors. Students make the product decisions: which problem, and for whom; what to cut once real users have tried it; and the name, the price, and the pitch. AI can't make those calls for them, and on demo day they explain every one of them live. That judgment is the part that compounds, and it's the part teachers already know how to coach.
