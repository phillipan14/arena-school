About Arena

Two builders, not career teachers.

Arena is run by its two co-founders. Neither of us trained as a teacher. We have spent our careers building with the tools that are now reshaping school, and we teach students to do the same.

The founding team

Phillip An

Co-founder

Caltech · McKinsey · Goldman Sachs · Harvard · Y Combinator · Schwarzman Scholars

Phillip studied computer science at Caltech, worked at McKinsey and Goldman Sachs, and earned an MBA and a master’s in engineering from Harvard. He co-founded Homebase, the first company from Vietnam backed by Y Combinator, and was a Schwarzman Scholar at Tsinghua. He designs and teaches every Arena program himself.

His parents were Chinese immigrants who cleaned houses in South Africa before they could move the family to America, so that he could go to the right schools. Their whole life was a bet on education, and it worked. Building a company through Y Combinator then showed him how people at the edge of technology actually learn: by building with tools no school teaches yet. Arena is his answer to a simple worry, that the path that worked for him will not work for the next generation.

International School of Beijing · Brown · Schwarzman Scholars · Kimi (Moonshot AI)

Jing Jing grew up at the International School of Beijing, from elementary school through high school graduation. Education became her passion through her younger sister. Jing Jing was closely involved in mapping out her sister’s path through school, and saw how much of it came down to knowing what was possible, long before any school said so. She is not an engineer, and she builds websites and tools with AI anyway. That is exactly what Arena teaches.

Why builders

Why two people who aren’t teachers started a school.

Neither of us has a teaching certificate. What we have is a clear view of where learning is already heading. In Silicon Valley, and inside the AI companies we have worked with, the people who move fastest don’t wait to be taught. They pick a problem, open the newest tools, and build until it works. That future of education is already visible there, years before it reaches most classrooms.

Schools will get there, but institutions move slower than the technology. So we bring that way of learning to students now: a real problem, real tools, a mentor in the room, and a demo day in front of people who build for a living.

The future of education

What’s left for humans: agency, taste, craft.

AI now handles the part of school that was about transferring knowledge. What it can’t do is decide what is worth building, judge whether it is good, or care enough to finish it.

Agency

The discipline to decide what to build next.

Not “follow the assignment.” Not “pick the right answer.” Look at the world and pick what to make.

In our first cohort, a ten-year-old picked sign language to speech, because his cousin is deaf. Nobody assigned it.

Taste

Knowing when the thing you made is good.

A rubric can tell you if you hit a target. Taste tells you if the target was worth hitting.

He kept iterating after the demo was over, because the first version wasn’t good enough for his grandmother to actually use.

Craft

The patience to finish, and to make it good.

AI gives you a first draft instantly. Craft is what you do with the next twenty drafts.

The hardest part wasn’t the model. It was getting the audio clean enough for an elderly woman to trust it.
