What teachers face

Four pressures, four answers.

The questions we hear from teachers before every session, and how each session is built around them.

  1. The pressure

    No time to learn one more thing

    Planning, grading, and differentiation already fill the week.

    How we answer it

    One task, one tool, in the session

    Each teacher brings a task from a class they teach now and leaves with a tool made with AI.

  2. The pressure

    Unclear rules on tools and data

    Which tools are allowed, and what student data may go into them, is often still being decided.

    How we answer it

    Your approved tools, your data rules

    We work only on the tools your school approves, and agree the data rules with your technology team first.

  3. The pressure

    Polished work that hides the thinking

    Students can hand in work AI helped produce, and the final product alone does not show who did the thinking.

    How we answer it

    Assess the process

    Drafts, reflections, and the reasons students give for each change, so the thinking is visible.

  4. The pressure

    Training that does not reach Monday

    A generic demo is hard to apply to the lesson you teach next week.

    How we answer it

    Start from one part of one class

    Teachers keep the tool and the prompt behind it, and each department gets a follow-up.

What the research says

Teachers are already using AI. The rules have not caught up.

Four findings from recent studies. Each links to its source.

  • 5.9hours

    a week saved, by their own estimate, among teachers who use AI at least weekly: about six weeks over a school year.

    Source: Gallup and Walton Family Foundation, survey of 2,232 U.S. public K-12 teachers, spring 2025

  • 31%

    less lesson-planning time for teachers in an English trial who used ChatGPT with a guide. An expert panel found no difference in quality.

    Source: Education Endowment Foundation trial, evaluated by NFER, reported March 2025

  • 53%

    of English, math, and science teachers said they used AI for school in 2025, up more than 15 points in one to two years.

    Source: RAND, AI Use in Schools Is Quickly Increasing but Guidance Lags Behind, September 2025

  • 34%

    of teachers reported school or district policies on AI use related to academic integrity.

    Source: RAND, AI Use in Schools Is Quickly Increasing but Guidance Lags Behind, September 2025

How we work with teachers

We start from what already works.

We don’t redesign your course. Each session starts from one small part of one class.

  1. 01 · Before

    Which tool do you wish existed?

    Each teacher brings one task from a class they teach now.

  2. 02 · Show

    One tool, live

    We make one tool on screen from a plain-language description.

  3. 03 · Make

    Your own tool

    Each teacher makes theirs on your approved tools and checks every result.

  4. 04 · Keep

    Ready for class

    Teachers keep the tool and the prompt behind it, to reuse and share.

Decisions about teaching and assessment stay with your faculty.

  • 01 · A staff meeting

    Live session

    We show one task live, then teachers try it on their own material.

  • 02 · A department

    Hands-on workshop

    Teachers work through several tasks on their own materials, with help in the room.

  • 03 · The whole faculty

    School-wide training

    Each department gets a session, then a follow-up.

Questions

Common questions.

Is this more work for me?

It starts from one task you already do, and the tool is made during the session. You decide whether to keep using it.

I’m not technical. Will I keep up?

Yes. No technical background or coding is needed.

What about students using AI to cheat?

We address it directly: how to assess the process, through drafts, reflections, and the reasons behind each change, and when AI doesn’t belong.

Our school restricts tools and student data.

We work only on the tools your school approves, and agree the data rules with your technology team first. For technology staff →

What do teachers keep afterward?

The tool they made and the prompt behind it, to reuse and share with colleagues.

Can it be for one department only?

Yes. A hands-on workshop for one department is one of the three formats; a school-wide training gives each department its own session and a follow-up.
