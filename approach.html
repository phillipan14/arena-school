Philosophy

Three human skills, so AI does not do the thinking.

When AI can write an essay or code in seconds, what must learners still do? Three skills that AI cannot supply, and that learners have to practice.

Agency

Choosing what is worth doing, before anyone assigns it.

Without it: AI picks the problem, and learners follow.

What it changes
  1. In schoolWhen AI can help finish an assignment, the harder skill is setting the question, not answering it.
  2. At workTasks that only follow instructions are the easiest to hand to AI; people who define the problem direct the work.
  3. Over a careerA project a learner chose and saw through shows initiative in a way a transcript cannot.

Taste

Judging whether the output is actually good.

Without it: Learners accept the first answer AI gives.

What it changes
  1. In schoolWhen every essay and project can look polished, telling strong work from weak becomes what teachers need to see.
  2. At workTeams can generate ten versions in an hour; the person who picks the right one, and can say why, is the one they rely on.
  3. Over a careerJudgment compounds: it is what earns trust with larger decisions over time.

Craft

Finishing the work so people can trust it.

Without it: The work stays an unchecked first draft.

What it changes
  1. In schoolA first draft is now instant; revising it into something reliable is where the learning happens.
  2. At workWorking products earn trust in a way prototypes and slide decks do not.
  3. Over a careerA record of finished work, with a live link, speaks for itself in applications and interviews.

This is how we teach every learner: students, teachers, school leaders, and parents.

Framework

The Arena School learning framework.

Learners do the thinking, AI is a tool, and teachers and peers give the feedback.

At the center, the learner does the thinking. We say learner on purpose: the same framework runs our student programs, our teacher training, and our sessions for school leaders and parents.

Four strands, in every program
  1. Inquiry and planning
  2. Research and evaluation
  3. Creating and communicating
  4. Collaboration and reflection
Four teaching principles
  1. In person, real-time feedback
  2. AI use disclosed and cited
  3. Learner voice and choice
  4. An authentic audience

Built on project-based learning, informed by PBLWorks’ Gold Standard PBL: every strand gets a real purpose and a real audience, and AI speeds up the building. At every check-in, the teacher asks How did you check it? and responds in real time. AI can support that feedback, not replace it.

Learning goals

Four domains, in every program.

The strands form a cycle, not a checklist: a project moves through all four, and back again.

  1. 01

    Inquiry and planning

    Turn an interest into a focused question, and plan the work.

  2. 02

    Research and evaluation

    Check sources, including AI output, and choose on evidence.

  3. 03

    Creating and communicating

    Build and test a product with AI, and explain it clearly.

  4. 04

    Collaboration and reflection

    Use critique from teachers and peers to revise the work.

Learning outcomes

What learners will be able to do.

Across every program, learners will be able to do the following.

Inquiry and planning

  • Turn a broad interest into a focused, researchable question.
  • Plan a project with milestones, and adjust the plan based on progress.

Research and evaluation

  • Find, evaluate, and cite sources, including checking the accuracy of AI output.
  • Compare options against criteria, and justify a choice.
  • Explain how AI tools work, where they fail, and the bias and privacy issues they raise.

Creating and communicating

  • Build and test a product, prototype, or solution, deciding which steps to hand to AI and disclosing how they used it.
  • Structure an argument and write it clearly, in their own words.
  • Present to an authentic audience, and defend their reasoning under questioning.

Collaboration and reflection

  • Work in a team, share roles, and contribute to a shared product.
  • Give and use critique from teachers and peers to revise their work.
  • Reflect on their process and on what they would do differently.

Learners are whoever a program is for: students, teachers, school leaders, or parents. The outcomes stay the same; the depth grows.

Assessment

We assess the thinking behind it.

We assess the process, not only the product: how a learner chose, checked, and finished the work.

Sample criterion · Research and evaluation

Evaluates sources, including AI output

  1. BeginningAccepts sources and AI output as given; cites inconsistently.
  2. DevelopingChecks some claims against a second source; cites most sources.
  3. ProficientVerifies key claims, including AI output, against reliable sources; discloses AI use.
  4. ExtendingExplains why sources disagree, corrects AI errors, and judges which evidence to trust.

In practice: used with students in the ISB AI Founders Program, summer 2026. With a school, the rubrics stay with its team.

Principles

How we teach, in every program.

Four teaching principles from the framework, the split between AI and the learner, and the eight principles behind the three skills.

  1. In person, real-time feedback

    Mentors and teachers sit with learners while they build, ask how they checked it, and respond on the spot.

  2. AI use disclosed and cited

    Learners decide which steps to hand to AI, and say how they used it.

  3. Learner voice and choice

    Learners choose the problem. Mentors support; they do not assign.

  4. An authentic audience

    Work goes in front of real users, judges, and families, not a simulated audience.

AI tools and mentors handle
  • Writing the code
  • Putting it online
  • Fixing technical errors
Learners decide
  • Which problem, and for whom
  • What to cut after users try it
  • The name, the price, and the pitch
The eight principles behind the three skills
  1. IAgency from day one. Mentors support; learners choose.
  2. IIMake things in front of people. Every day ends with a demo.
  3. IIIExperienced mentors in the room. Mentors are founders, engineers, and designers who have made real products.
  4. IVAI as a partner learners direct. Learners write the prompts, question the output, and check it for mistakes.
  5. VReflection, started early. Thirty screen-free minutes a day on one question: which of my goals are my own?
  6. VIRisk early, while it’s cheap. Small, repeated risks make larger ones easier.
  7. VIIDaily habits, personal systems. A three-line update every day: finished, learned, next.
  8. VIIIFirst-principles thinking. Learners are assessed on asking how, why, what principle, when it fails, and how to adapt.
The rhythm of a program day

The same rhythm runs in every format, from weekend sessions to five-day intensives.

  1. StartLesson

    Taught right before learners need it.

  2. Most of the dayBuild

    On a problem the team chose.

  3. During the buildMentor 1:1s

    Mentors sit with teams when they are stuck.

  4. Fixed hourThe freeze

    Building stops; teams demo what works.

  5. DailyReflection

    Thirty minutes with a notebook, no screens.

  6. End of dayStandup

    Finished, learned, and next.

  7. Last dayDemo day

    A live pitch to judges and families.
