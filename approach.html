Our approach

Agency, taste, and craft.

When AI can make almost anything, students need to know what’s worth making, whether it’s good, and how to finish it.

Find the right program →See results
Three pillars

How students practice each one.

On projects they choose, with mentors in the room.

Agency

Choosing what’s worth solving.

AI can help make almost anything, so the real work is deciding what’s worth making. Students choose their project on day one.

  • Pitch their own idea in three minutes, no slides.
  • Ask an expert for fifteen minutes of advice.
  • Set a personal goal to keep for ninety days.

What it changes

  1. In schoolWhen AI can complete an assignment, the harder skill is setting the question, not answering it.
  2. At workTasks that only follow instructions are the easiest to hand to AI; people who define the problem direct the work.
  3. Over a careerA project a student chose and saw through shows initiative in a way a transcript cannot.
The principles behind it
  1. IAgency from day one. Mentors support; students choose.
  2. VReflection, started early. Thirty screen-free minutes a day on one question: which of my goals are my own?
  3. VIRisk early, while it’s cheap. Small, repeated risks make larger ones easier.

Taste

Telling which version actually works.

AI helps students try many versions. Showing work to users and mentors every day teaches them which one works.

  • Put a prototype online at a public link by midway.
  • Work through the product with an experienced mentor.
  • Test it with five questions, then defend each answer.

What it changes

  1. In schoolWhen every essay and project can look polished, telling strong work from weak becomes what teachers need to see.
  2. At workTeams can generate ten versions in an hour; the person who picks the right one, and can say why, is the one they rely on.
  3. Over a careerJudgment compounds: it is what earns trust with larger decisions over time.
The principles behind it
  1. IIMake things in front of people. Every day ends with a demo.
  2. IIIExperienced mentors in the room. Mentors are founders, engineers, and designers who have made real products.
  3. VIIIFirst-principles thinking. Students are assessed on asking how, why, what principle, when it fails, and how to adapt.

Craft

Finishing it well.

AI helps students reach a first draft sooner; finishing a reliable product is their own work. Every build ends in a live demo.

  • Build with AI coding tools, making every call.
  • Stop at a fixed freeze and demo what works.
  • Write a one-page personal routine to take home.

What it changes

  1. In schoolA first draft is now instant; revising it into something reliable is where the learning happens.
  2. At workWorking products earn trust in a way prototypes and slide decks do not.
  3. Over a careerA record of finished work, with a live link, speaks for itself in applications and interviews.
The principles behind it
  1. IVAI as a partner students direct. Students write the prompts, question the output, and check it for mistakes.
  2. VIIDaily habits, personal systems. A three-line update every day: finished, learned, next.
How a program runs

A typical day, start to finish.

The same rhythm runs in every format, from weekend sessions to five-day intensives.

  1. StartLesson

    Taught right before students need it.

  2. Most of the dayBuild

    On a problem the team chose.

  3. During the buildMentor 1:1s

    Mentors sit with teams when they are stuck.

  4. Fixed hourThe freeze

    Building stops; teams demo what works.

  5. DailyReflection

    Thirty minutes with a notebook, no screens.

  6. End of dayStandup

    Finished, learned, and next.

  7. Last dayDemo day

    A live pitch to judges and parents.

Formats
  • Five-day intensives in school breaks
  • Weekends and after school
  • Partner cohorts at host schools
Students take home

A working product with their name on it, a personal routine they wrote, professionals who know them by name, and experience presenting in public.
